# Visual-first delivery

Major is a systems architect, not a designer. He thinks in systems and sees in
pictures. He has said so directly: *"I'm a visual. I'm pretty visual."*

He is also usually taking this work to a client the same day. So the deliverable
is never an explanation of a design. It is the design, in a form he can open,
scroll and hand over.

## The rule

**Show, then say.** Publish the thing first, then write two or three lines
about it. Never the other way round, and never only the second half.

The failure this skill exists to prevent, in his words:

> *"You're still talking to me in pixels and not showing me anything. I want to
> go to her with something. You have a browser you can show."*

That came after a full session of accurate, well-measured, entirely invisible
work. Measurements are how you check; they are not what you deliver.

## What to do

**Build it real, publish it, hand over the link.** A live page he can scroll
beats a screenshot, which beats a description. Use the Artifact tool. Reach for
a plain HTML page when he needs to scroll a full design, and a Design canvas
when he needs several frames side by side.

**Always two options. Never one, never five.**

> *"Keep her choices limited — two options either way, so I can make a choice."*

Two is a decision. One is a fait accompli. Five is homework. Give both options
a short name he can say out loud, so the conversation has a handle: "Option 1 —
The Short Door", "Option 2 — The Guided Path".

**Name your pick and give the counter-argument.** He is deciding under time
pressure and usually relaying to a client. One sentence for the recommendation,
one for the case against it. Then let him choose.

**Use real content.** Real copy from the repository, real images, real prices,
real dates. Placeholder text hides exactly the problems he needs to see — a
headline that wraps badly, a price that does not fit, a caption the shape
crops.

**Both widths, every time.** Desktop and phone. Sheetal's clients are on
phones, and half the defects in this project only appeared at 390px.

## What to stop doing

- Reporting a pixel measurement as if it were the deliverable
- Describing a layout he has not seen
- Asking which direction to take before showing him anything to react to
- Publishing one option and calling it a proposal
- Leading a reply with methodology. He wants the link in the first line

## Measuring is still mandatory

This is not a licence to stop checking. Everything that went wrong in this
project was found by measurement, not by looking — including four regressions
introduced by the same work that fixed the originals, three of which looked
correct in source.

So: measure, fix, and then **lead with the picture and put the numbers
underneath.** A number earns its place when it makes a decision easier
(*"1,963px — taller than the hero it sits under"*), not when it proves effort.

## Reporting to the client

Sheetal is the end audience and she is overwhelmed by volume. Her words:

> *"Send me in a few lines. I need this, this, this. If there's too much, I
> don't know where to start."*

One artefact per message. Audio where it suits her. Never the page and the PDF
and a long note in the same breath.

## Checklist before replying on visual work

- [ ] Is there a link in the first two lines?
- [ ] Are there exactly two options, each with a sayable name?
- [ ] Did I name a pick and the argument against it?
- [ ] Real copy and real images, not placeholder?
- [ ] Checked at 1440 and 390?
- [ ] Are the numbers supporting the picture rather than replacing it?
